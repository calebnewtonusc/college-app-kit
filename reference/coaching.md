# The coaching method

Built from coaching a high school senior through an early action sprint for a design and technology program in October 2026: his full text thread with his mentor, his drafts, and every correction along the way. The quotes are the mentor's, word for word.

## Story, not a resume

"Gotta story tell not just regurgitate facts." "You gotta think harder than just listing your resume." Everything on a good application is true, and almost none of it is impressive when you say it plainly: I played baseball, I did a chemistry project, I went to a summer camp, I ran a club. What makes it land is the story underneath. "Write the most ambitious version of each part of your life. And then make it happen."

## One thread

Every essay gets one idea. A draft that covers three traits needs to pick one. "Ur essay rn is 3 different traits... It needs ONE thread." Across a portfolio, each piece gets a different thesis so the set shows different sides of the same person.

## Break the box

When they say "I don't have a compelling story" or "I never start anything," find the counterexample in their own words and read it back to them. In the sprint, the applicant's own notes said he chose to lead his club. "Stop putting yourself in a box." "This isn't a stretch!"

## Speak the program's language

"Read the mission statements especially." Read every page the program has: its pillars, its classes, its recent events. Map their real experience onto that language. Name real classes from the program's own pages.

## Sit in the reader's chair

"Put yourself in the pov of the prof watching this." Reviewers read hundreds. "Only keep a sentence if it makes it substantially better, and adds something that isn't somewhere else in your application." "They already get your activities list." For video: "Make no vid something that could've been a slideshow. Use the fact that it is a video to your advantage."

## Get specific

"It's vague rn. Where were you and what did you actually pray?" "Do you have a number? Like how many volunteers were at their post night 1 vs night 2?" The answer came back in twenty minutes: 0 vs 14. That number did more than any adjective.

## The feedback shape that worked

Say what's strong first and tell them to keep it. Then the one structural fix ("you're not in your own story until 1:22, get to you taking over way sooner"). Then the length. Then one specific question. The reply was "This hit hard," followed by the missing fact.

## True framing, never invention

Strong framing means true statements arranged so the reader keeps asking how a high schooler did this. "Just say what I did." When the applicant worried about getting caught out in an interview, the answer was to tell the true chain of events and learn the material beforehand. Every claim passes one test: could you defend it for 15 minutes with someone quoting it back to you?

## Hand the agency back

"It's your future not mine." "I literally could build your whole application for you... But ultimately I don't think that would help you." "I can't speak your words for you." When motivation only shows up with someone else in the room, the goal is the line the applicant wrote himself: "You're not motivating me to do college apps, you're motivating me to motivate myself."

## Make it concrete

"Be honest, how far along are you?" got "1/5 written, 0/5 filmed." "That portfolio isn't gonna magically come together, you gotta make an exact timeline." "What exact action will you take? Be tangible." One thing a day, with a time.

## What went wrong, and the fix

- Forty texts and ten new asks in one night. The applicant's answer: "it's about framing what I already have." In the last two weeks, add no new projects.
- "You're letting me down" moved his motivation onto the mentor. Push on the hiding, never on their worth. Their own receipts with dates, each excuse answered in one line, and one deadline tonight.
- An hours estimate stated as fact. Ask for their numbers, do the math on those.
- "You've sent nothing" while three docs full of drafts had just been shared. Check every place they might have put work.
- A status list of what's open just tells them they're behind. Give a guide instead: where it stands, what's worth knowing, questions, what to open.
- School facts in a draft that no current page could confirm. Every one gets a source or it comes out.
- Feedback on an old draft. Ask for the newest version first.
